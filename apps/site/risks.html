Risks

Fifteen ways this hurts you, and what is done about each.

Seven of these have no mitigation and are simply accepted. They are marked as accepted rather than buried, because a risk page that only lists solved problems is a marketing page wearing a warning label.

Structural — no mitigation exists

1. Immutability itself

What it is
As written, the contracts contain no proxy, no upgrade path, no pause and no admin key. Whatever ships is what runs, permanently. This describes source code that has not been deployed. There is no deployed bytecode against which any of it can be independently verified today.
Worst case
A critical bug that survived review is permanent. Funds may be unrecoverable, and no party — including the people who wrote the code — has any power to stop it, patch it or claw anything back.
What is done
Nothing can be done after deployment; that is the point. Before then: an external security review whose report is held privately, with no public report to check it against, four internal adversarial review rounds, and an AI pre-audit. The evidence that this is not enough is in the project's own history — the AI pre-audit found five Critical issues in a frozen tree that had already passed internal review, and every fix required a full redeploy because nothing can be edited in place.
Accepted by design

2. An oracle freeze traps every exit

What it is
If a price feed goes past its staleness bound, the oracle refuses to answer and every function that reads NAV reverts: deposits, rebalance execution and redemptions alike.
Worst case
All active capital in the vault is frozen for as long as the feed stays past its bound, with exits included and no escape hatch. You cannot leave at any price while the freeze lasts. If the feed is deprecated or retired rather than merely late, the freeze does not end: a vault's oracle cannot be replaced, which is risk 15.
What is done
Nothing, deliberately, and no hatch will be added — any escape hatch is exactly the stale-price exit the breaker exists to prevent. The reasoning is that freezing beats mispricing: an exit priced off a stale feed is a transfer from the members who stay to whoever moves first. The one thing that stays reclaimable during a freeze is un-activated observation-window capital, because cancelling a pending deposit reads no oracle.
Partially mitigated

3. A single price provider, with no fallback

What it is
Each asset is priced from exactly one Chainlink Data Feed: WETH from ETH/USD, cbBTC from BTC/USD. There is no second provider and no fallback source. This is a named residual risk, not an oversight.
Worst case
A wrong answer that lands inside the sane-price band and inside the heartbeat is treated as truth, and the vault trades or settles on it. A feed deprecation fails that asset closed, which stops the vault rather than mispricing it — but there is nothing to fail over to.
What is done
Three defences, and they are the only ones: a heartbeat and staleness bound per asset (3,600 seconds in the reference mainnet configuration), a plausibility band per asset that rejects prices outside it, and the mandatory Base sequencer gate. The band is wide — in the reference configuration $100 to $100,000 for WETH and $1,000 to $1,000,000 for cbBTC. It rejects gross errors. It does not reject an adverse but plausible price. The basket is limited to the two assets Base has Chainlink USD feeds for, rather than reaching for assets that would need a weaker price source.
Accepted — no mitigation

4. USDC is pinned at $1.00 in the oracle

What it is
The oracle does not price USDC. It assumes one dollar.
Worst case
A sustained USDC depeg is mispriced by exactly the size of the depeg. Every NAV computed during it is wrong by that amount, and every deposit, redemption and rebalance priced off that NAV is wrong with it. There is no median and no second source to outvote the pin.
What is done
Nothing. This one is accepted outright. If you think a sustained depeg is likely, this protocol misprices your position for the whole duration and you should treat that as disqualifying.
Handled in code, never exercised

5. Sequencer downtime

What it is
Base is an L2 with a sequencer. When it stops, on-chain prices stop being trustworthy before they stop being available.
Worst case
A sequencer incident extends into a vault freeze that outlasts the incident itself, because the oracle will not price anything until the grace period has elapsed.
What is done
A Chainlink L2 Sequencer Uptime Feed is mandatory — without one the oracle reverts every price — and it enforces a 3,600-second grace period after the sequencer comes back up. The mitigation and the risk are the same mechanism: you are protected from stale-sequencer pricing by being locked out for an hour longer than the outage. This path has never executed against a real sequencer feed. Testnet leaves the feed address at zero by design, so its first real execution would be on mainnet.
Accepted by design

6. Forward-settled exits are irrevocable

What it is
From the moment the reveal phase opens on any live proposal — not from the moment one passes — redemption requests queue in forward settlement mode and price at post-execution NAV. The window lasts until that proposal executes, is defeated, or its execution window lapses. A proposal that is ultimately defeated still forced your exit into the queue while it was live.
Worst case
You can be right about disliking a rebalance, vote against it, request your exit, and still carry the full effect of its execution. Once queued, the request cannot be withdrawn. Your shares stay outstanding and keep gaining and losing with the vault, but they are locked: non-transferable and excluded from voting-eligible stake. There is no cap on how often this window recurs. One passed proposal holds it for the vault's timelock plus its execution window — 48 hours in the reference configuration, and up to about 31 days at the protocol's hard caps. A member holding a single minimum deposit can re-open proposals to hold exits in this mode roughly half the time, indefinitely, for the cost of gas.
What is done
Nothing, deliberately. The alternative is worse: instant exits during that window would be a free option to leave at pre-rebalance prices while already knowing the outcome, paid for by every member who stayed. The recurrence is a known, accepted, unmitigated finding (M-7): the proposal cooldown raises its cost and does not remove it, because the cooldown is keyed per proposer and a second address sidesteps it. If the proposal is defeated or its execution window lapses without execution, a queued exit settles at the NAV current at settlement — but settlement is not automatic in that case. settleQueuedExit has to be called, and anyone can call it. Separately, requestExit takes no minimum-value parameter: your exit is an unbounded market order against whatever NAV settlement produces, there is no transaction-level floor you can set, and one was deliberately dropped for contract-size reasons. The operator's rebalance swaps do carry a minimum-out bound. Your principal does not.
Partially mitigated

7. Governance capture and thin electorates

What it is
Voting is stake-weighted at five or more members, so a large enough holder can pass proposals. Below five members the vault takes a different branch: a proposal passes on either a majority of the members-at-creation revealing in favour while the favouring stake still clears the quorum, or an outright favouring stake majority. Both branches are stake-sensitive, so neither is a pure head count, and a rule change instead requires full consensus of eligible stake.
Worst case
A small vault is governed by whoever holds the most. In the small-member regime the residual is not a cheap head-count flip: because both sub-five branches weigh stake, dust addresses cannot pass a proposal on numbers alone. What is still purchasable is the regime itself — buying seats up to five moves the vault out of the signer-count branch and into the pure stake rule, and a seat costs one minimum deposit. At the reference 100 USDC minimum deposit that is about 400 USDC for the four seats a single-member vault needs. There is no contract-level floor here — the minimum deposit is chosen by whoever created the vault, and a low one makes capture cheap. Check it before you deposit. Proposals you oppose pass, and your exit settles after they execute.
What is done
A protocol quorum floor of 25% of voting-eligible stake, a per-vault proposal threshold, a cap on how much delegated weight any single delegate may receive, and a minimum deposit that exists precisely as a listing constraint against cheap address-splitting in the small-member regime. There is also a per-proposer cooldown, which is not a defence against this: it is keyed per proposer, so a second address sidesteps it entirely. None of these makes a determined majority holder harmless, and the purchasable member count below five members is the open High-severity finding at the launch configuration.
Accepted by design

8. The rules can freeze permanently

What it is
After a vault is funded, changing its rules requires 100% of voting-eligible stake plus a timelock.
Worst case
One permanently offline member — a lost key, a death, a walked-away wallet — freezes the vault's rules forever. Nobody can change a parameter again, no matter how much everyone else agrees.
What is done
Nothing. Unanimity is what makes "nobody can change the rules behind you" true, and a lower bar would make it false. The consequence is stated here rather than discovered later.
Accepted — no recovery path

9. Operator identity cannot be rotated

What it is
The operator identity is attested by the registry at vault creation and is immutable for that vault. There is no rebind, and the payout address is permanent.
Worst case
A compromised operator identity cannot be rotated, replaced or revoked. Whoever controls it controls the proposal right that identity carries, for as long as the vault exists.
What is done
The remedy is procedural, not technical: wind the vault down through exits and launch a new one. The compromise cannot let an attacker move member funds — the operator has no such power — but it cannot be undone either. Operators are told to use a multisig, because the address is permanent.
Not mitigable

10. Total loss is possible

What it is
The vault holds spot crypto assets. Spot crypto assets fall, sometimes a long way, sometimes permanently.
Worst case
You lose everything you deposited. Market losses, a permanent bug, or both.
What is done
Nothing, and nothing can be. There is no insurance fund, no backstop, no reimbursement and no party who makes anyone whole. Do not deposit what you cannot afford to lose entirely.
Open — unresolved

11. Securities and collective investment scheme recharacterization

What it is
Interests in these vaults may be treated as securities, or as collective investment scheme interests, in some jurisdictions. This is an unresolved legal question, not a settled one.
Worst case
A regulator takes that view. Participants face consequences that depend entirely on where they are, and this project cannot indemnify anyone against them.
What is done
Disclosure, and an intended geofence. Nothing on this site is an offer, a solicitation, or financial advice, and no part of it is intended to induce anyone to part with money. Access from restricted jurisdictions is intended to be geofenced at the front end — a good-faith measure and not a guarantee, because the contracts are permissionless and can be called directly by anyone, with or without a front end. Take your own advice about your own jurisdiction. We will not characterize your position for you.
Out of review scope

12. The reference agent is beta code

What it is
A reference operator agent ships in the repository. It is beta reference code and it sat outside the scope of the contract security review.
Worst case
An operator runs it as-is, it proposes something the operator did not intend, and the members pass it.
What is done
It is labelled as reference code here and in the repository. It holds no protocol-level privilege — in governance it can only propose and vote its own weight — so its failure mode is a bad proposal, not a theft. Read it before you run it.
Open — with counsel

13. An open licensing question

What it is
Vendored third-party mathematics is under GPL-2.0-or-later and MIT terms inside a BUSL-1.1 repository.
Worst case
The combination is judged incompatible, and the licensing of part of the tree has to change.
What is done
It is flagged for counsel and recorded rather than quietly left alone. It is disclosed here because you should know about it before you build on the repository, not after.
Structural

14. This is experimental software

What it is
New contracts, a new governance mechanism, a new operator model, none of it battle-tested by time or volume, and none of it deployed anywhere yet.
Worst case
Something nobody on this page thought of.
What is done
The first vault's planned capacity cap is 50,000 USDC, a deliberate limit on how much any single unknown can destroy. It is a planned parameter of a vault that has not been created. That is a bound on the blast radius, not a defence. Do not deposit what you cannot afford to lose entirely.
Accepted — no recovery path

15. There is no oracle rotation path

What it is
Each vault's oracle address is fixed in immutable code at construction. Governance has no oracle-shaped proposal. The factory's allowlist is fixed in its constructor with no add, no remove and no owner, and it governs only which oracle a new vault may be created with.
Worst case
If a feed is deprecated or permanently retired, the freeze in risk 2 is permanent. Members are locked out for good, with the funds visibly on-chain and no party able to change it — not the operator, not the members, not the deployers. A replacement oracle cannot be attached to an existing vault; it can only be blessed for new vaults on a new factory, and existing vaults keep pricing through the dead one.
What is done
Nothing, deliberately. The alternative is an address able to bless a new oracle, which is an address able to bless a fake price feed — the exact attack the allowlist exists to close, and it would be the protocol's first standing privileged role. Creating a new vault against a dead feed fails loudly rather than producing a brick, so the failure mode is "that asset becomes unlistable until a new factory is published", never "broken vaults ship". Publishing a new factory also restarts the operator registry, the leaderboard and the loss carryforward in a fresh registry.

On the security review

What has and has not been checked.

An external security review was commissioned against the launch tree. The owner attests it returned no major issues. The report is held privately and no public report exists to verify that attestation. Alongside it, four internal adversarial review rounds and an AI pre-audit were run; the AI pre-audit found 41 issues including 5 Critical. All five Criticals are resolved or closed by launch configuration. One High — the purchasable member count below five members — remains open at the launch configuration, and a set of Medium and Low findings are accepted residuals that will not be fixed. A further class is dormant only because sub-vaults are disabled at launch: those are not repaired in code and would return if sub-vaults were ever enabled.

The remaining launch blockers are operational and legal rather than security: a testnet lifecycle re-run, a soak, a canary re-run, a recorded restore drill, and counsel review. Every security gate the team defined is cleared. That still certifies that the gates ran, not that the protocol is secure. One High — the purchasable member count below five members — remains open at the launch configuration and is not classified as a launch blocker. The launch verdict as at 2026-08-29 is still NO-GO, recorded at protocol/main; check the repository for anything later.
